## 🎤 Speaker Notes (what you say — ~3 minutes)

> *"Now to the second half of the Big Four — **Iran and North Korea**. If China and Russia are the strategic chess players, Iran and North Korea are the opportunists. And that makes them, in some ways, harder to predict."*
>
> **[Turn to the Iran card]**
> *"**Iran** is defined by one word: **volatility**.*
>
> *Their technical sophistication is medium — not at the level of China or Russia. But what they lack in finesse, they compensate for with **aggression and speed** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). When there is a regional flashpoint — a strike in the Gulf, a diplomatic incident, a sanctions escalation — Iranian cyber activity spikes within days, sometimes hours.*
>
> *Their signature move is the **wiper attack combined with a public leak** — what we call 'hack-and-leak' [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). They don't just destroy your data. They **publish stolen documents** to humiliate you, to influence public opinion, to demonstrate reach. It's cyber operations as **theater**, aimed at psychological impact as much as operational damage.*
>
> *The clearest recent example is **Handala's attack on Stryker** — the case I opened this presentation with. Two hundred thousand devices wiped across 79 countries [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). Stryker wasn't a political target. It was a **medical equipment manufacturer**. That is the point I want to hammer home: **Iranian retaliation does not require you to be politically involved.** Being a Western company in the wrong sector at the wrong moment is sufficient."*
>
> **[Turn to the North Korea card]**
> *"**North Korea** is the outlier — and the most misunderstood.*
>
> *Every other nation-state in this presentation is motivated by **strategic goals** — power, dominance, disruption. North Korea is motivated by **money**. Full stop [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md).*
>
> *The regime is under crushing international sanctions. Cyber operations have become one of its primary sources of hard currency — funding its nuclear and missile programs directly. This makes North Korea **structurally unique**: they operate like an **elite cybercrime syndicate**, but with the resources, protection, and legal immunity of a state [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md).*
>
> *Their preferred targets follow the money: **cryptocurrency exchanges** where funds are hardest to recover, **banks**, and increasingly **defense supply chains** — because they can steal IP and sell it or use it.*
>
> *The **Lazarus Group** case from late 2025 is textbook. In a single quarter, they stole **$30.4 million** in cryptocurrency from South Korea's Upbit exchange — that's direct funding for the regime. **And simultaneously**, they infiltrated three European defense companies to steal drone manufacturing IP [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). One operation, two revenue streams. That level of operational efficiency is what makes them exceptionally dangerous to any financial institution."*
>
> **[Deliver the bottom banner slowly]**
> *"So — four actors, four completely different motivations. But the consequence for us is uniform: **no private-sector target is out of scope anymore**. Stryker was medical equipment. Upbit was crypto. Singapore's telecoms were civilian infrastructure. If your sector is adjacent to geopolitics, finance, or supply chains — you're on the map."*

## 💡 Anticipated ExCo Questions (be ready)

**Q1: "As a bank, aren't we North Korea's dream target?"**
> Suggested answer: *"Yes — and we treat them accordingly. Lazarus and its subsidiaries have specifically targeted SWIFT infrastructure, cryptocurrency custody, and correspondent banking flows for years. Our SWIFT controls, transaction monitoring, and crypto-exposure risk framework are calibrated with **North Korea as the top-tier financial adversary**. What has changed recently is their **speed** — they now move stolen funds through mixers and cross-chain bridges in minutes, not hours. Our recovery window has essentially collapsed."*

**Q2: "Iran attacked a medical device company. Why would they attack *us*?"**
> Suggested answer: *"Two vectors of exposure. First, **sanctions enforcement** — every Western bank that enforces Iran sanctions is a symbolic target for retaliation. Second, **client exposure** — we finance sectors Iran considers adversarial: defense, energy, regional geopolitical actors. We don't need to be the primary target to become collateral in a broader campaign."*

**Q3: "You said Iran's sophistication is medium. Should we worry less about them than about China?"**
> Suggested answer: *"No — and this is a common trap. **Sophistication is not the same as impact.** Iran's Stryker attack was technically not extraordinary — but it wiped 200,000 devices [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). A mid-sophistication actor with **aggressive intent and low risk-aversion** can do more damage than a highly sophisticated actor operating cautiously. In risk terms, we care about **impact × likelihood** — and Iran scores high on both."*

**Q4: "Is North Korea working with Russia now? I've seen headlines."**
> Suggested answer: *"There's clear evidence of **transfer of tools and techniques** between the two, especially in the wake of the Ukraine war. Russia benefits from North Korean cybercrime infrastructure for deniability; North Korea benefits from Russian tradecraft. It's not a formal alliance, but it's a **compound risk** — the capabilities of one become available to the other."*

**Q5: "How do we prioritize among four different actor types?"** *(the resource-allocation question)*
> Suggested answer: *"We don't pick one. Our defense architecture has to be **actor-agnostic at the technical layer** — good hygiene, Zero-Trust, EDR everywhere — and **actor-informed at the strategic layer** — meaning our threat intelligence, our SOC playbooks, and our crisis scenarios are tailored to each of the four. Same defenses, different response postures."*
