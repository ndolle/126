# 🎯 Slide 9 — Prospective Vision: The Escalation of Offensive AI

This slide shifts the tension from **"what has happened"** to **"what is coming next."** After 8 slides of grounded evidence, ExCo needs a forward-looking moment — but one that stays credible and actionable, not science-fiction. The narrative discipline: **three concrete near-future risks, each with a specific business consequence** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md).

## 🖼 On-Slide Content

### **Title (top):**
> # Prospective Vision
> ### *The Escalation of Offensive AI — What's coming in the next 12-18 months*

### **Opening framing statement (top band, high visual weight):**
> **Artificial Intelligence now equips threat actors with unprecedented capabilities for adaptation and massive automation.** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md)

### **Layout — Three horizontal "risk cards" stacked vertically:**

---

#### 🎣 **RISK 1 — AUTOMATED SPEAR-PHISHING AT SCALE**
> ### *The end of "spot the phishing email"*

**The capability:**
Massive generation of **flawless, hyper-targeted fraudulent emails** using LLMs [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md) — personalized to the target's role, communication style, active projects, and recent LinkedIn activity.

**What changes:**
- **Volume:** thousands of unique, tailored campaigns per adversary per day
- **Quality:** grammatically perfect, contextually accurate in any language
- **Cost per attack:** collapses from ~€50 (human effort) to effectively €0

**Business consequence:**
> *Employee training and "look for red flags" awareness campaigns become structurally obsolete. Technical controls must assume every phishing email will be convincing.*

---

#### 🧬 **RISK 2 — AUTONOMOUS MALWARE AGENTS**
> ### *Malware that thinks*

**The capability:**
**Smart malware capable of dynamically modifying its tactics in real time to evade defenses** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md) — AI-driven decision loops embedded in the malicious code itself.

**What changes:**
- Malware **learns from your defenses** during the intrusion — adapting evasion techniques as it encounters detection
- **No two infections behave identically** — signature-based defense loses even more ground
- Human incident responders face a **reasoning adversary**, not a static payload

**Business consequence:**
> *Detection and response cycles compress from hours to minutes. Automated response capability moves from "nice to have" to "prerequisite."*

---

#### 🎭 **RISK 3 — DEEPFAKES & EXECUTIVE FRAUD**
> ### *When the CEO on the call isn't the CEO*

**The capability:**
**Voice and video cloning of corporate executives** to facilitate major financial fraud [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md) — real-time, interactive, indistinguishable from the real person over a video call.

**What changes:**
- CFO receives a **live video call from the CEO** authorizing an urgent wire transfer — the caller is synthetic
- Voice-only variants already documented in **multi-million-euro losses** at peer institutions *[industry benchmark]*
- Trust in **any digital channel** — email, voice, video — degrades simultaneously

**Business consequence:**
> *Financial authorization workflows must be redesigned around "channel-independent verification." The identity of the sender is no longer a control.*

---

### **Bottom banner (spanning the slide):**
> **AI does not create new categories of attack. It removes the human bottleneck from existing ones — collapsing cost, scale, and detection windows simultaneously.**

## 🎤 Speaker Notes (what you say — ~2-3 minutes)

> *"Everything I've shown you so far is what has already happened. I want to close the threat picture with a brief look at the next 12 to 18 months — because the pace of AI-driven capability development means these are not distant scenarios. They are operational realities being weaponized right now."*
>
> **[Point to the framing statement]**
> *"The core message is this: **AI now equips threat actors with unprecedented capabilities for adaptation and massive automation** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md). It's important to understand what that means precisely. **AI does not create new categories of attack.** Spear-phishing existed. Adaptive malware existed. Executive impersonation fraud existed. What AI does is **remove the human bottleneck** from all of them — collapsing cost, scale, and detection windows simultaneously.*
>
> *Three specific risks I want to put on your radar."*
>
> **[Point to Risk 1 — Automated Spear-Phishing]**
> *"**First: automated spear-phishing at scale** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md).*
>
> *Twelve months ago, a convincing spear-phishing email — the kind personalized enough to fool a senior executive — took an attacker roughly a day of research to craft. Today, an LLM does it in seconds, in any language, referencing your actual LinkedIn posts, your active M&A rumors, your team members by name.*
>
> *The consequence for us is uncomfortable: **our awareness training investments are structurally losing value**. We can no longer tell employees to 'look for the red flags' because the red flags are gone. We have to shift budget from **human vigilance** to **technical controls** — email authentication, link isolation, behavioral analysis of unusual requests. The 'trained employee' is no longer our last line of defense; the system has to be."*
>
> **[Point to Risk 2 — Autonomous Agents]**
> *"**Second: autonomous malware agents** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md).*
>
> *This one is more technical but strategically important. Traditional malware executes a pre-programmed set of actions. **Autonomous malware carries an AI decision loop with it** — it observes what defenses it encounters and adapts. If your EDR blocks one technique, the malware tries another. If it detects a sandbox, it lies dormant. If it finds a specific type of environment, it activates a tailored payload.*
>
> *For our defenders, this means the **response window shrinks**. Human incident responders working through decision trees will lose to malware that iterates faster than they can. **This is why automated response capability — SOAR, Security Orchestration and Automated Response — moves from optional to essential.**"*
>
> **[Point to Risk 3 — Deepfakes]**
> *"**Third: deepfakes and executive fraud** [[1]](file://Pres-geoPolitique.md) — and this is the one that lands closest to this room.*
>
> *The technology to clone an executive's voice from three minutes of public audio has been mature for a year. **The technology to conduct a live, interactive video call as another person is now here.** Peer institutions have already reported multi-million-euro losses from voice-only variants of this attack — where a CFO received an urgent call from someone who sounded exactly like the CEO.*
>
> *The implication for our controls is direct. **Financial authorization workflows that rely on "recognizing the person" — voice, video, or even direct message — are no longer safe.** Any high-value authorization must go through a **channel-independent verification** step. Not because we don't trust our executives — but because we can no longer trust the channel."*
>
> **[Deliver the bottom banner slowly]**
> *"So — to close this section — **AI is not inventing new threats. It is industrializing the ones we already knew.** And it is compressing the timelines within which our defenses have to operate. Every control we have that depends on human judgment in the loop — spotting a phishing email, recognizing a voice, reacting to a fast-moving intrusion — is now on the wrong side of the automation curve.*
>
> *This is the operational context in which the resilience strategy I'll close with must operate."*

## 💡 Anticipated ExCo Questions (be ready)

**Q1: "Has BNP Paribas experienced a deepfake attempt yet?"** *(the direct one)*
> Suggested answer: *"To my knowledge, we have observed **voice-cloning attempts** against mid-level finance staff in the past 12 months — none of which succeeded, thanks to our existing dual-authorization controls. We have not yet detected a **video-based** attempt, though we assess it as a matter of when, not if. Our red team is currently building deepfake scenarios into our next executive tabletop exercise."*

**Q2: "You said awareness training is losing value. Should we stop training people?"**
> Suggested answer: *"No — we should **change what we train on**. Traditional 'spot the phishing email' training is losing value. What retains value is **process training** — teaching employees to verify unusual requests through independent channels, regardless of how convincing the request looks. The shift is from 'detect the fake' to 'verify the legitimate.' Same budget envelope, different content."*

**Q3: "Can we use AI to defend, or is this all one-sided?"** *(the optimistic question)*
> Suggested answer: *"Absolutely — and we already do. AI in defense powers our behavioral analytics, our anomaly detection, and increasingly our automated response. The honest picture is that **AI helps both sides, but the attacker gets faster leverage** from it in the short term. Attack requires one success; defense requires perfect coverage. AI amplifies both, but the asymmetry favors offense initially. That gap closes over time — but 'over time' is measured in years, and we have to defend today."*

**Q4: "Are these AI risks being addressed by regulators?"**
> Suggested answer: *"Yes, in fragmented ways. The **EU AI Act** covers certain deployment scenarios; **DORA** implicitly covers AI-augmented operational risk in finance; **NIS2** touches supply chain implications. But there is no consolidated regulatory framework for AI-driven cyber threats yet. In the interim, industry consortia — including the FS-ISAC — are building shared intelligence on AI-enabled attacks. We participate actively."*

**Q5: "This all sounds inevitable. What is actually within our control?"** *(the agency question)*
> Suggested answer: *"Three things, and all three are within our control. **First**, we can invest in the technical controls that don't depend on human judgment — email authentication, transaction verification workflows, automated response. **Second**, we can redesign our critical processes — especially financial authorization — around channel-independent verification. **Third**, we can rehearse. The organizations that will handle AI-enabled attacks best are the ones that have already war-gamed them. Our next executive tabletop should include a deepfake scenario, and I'd like the committee's support on that."*

**Q6: "How real is the deepfake video threat today, versus 6 months from now?"**
> Suggested answer: *"**Real today** for asynchronous scenarios — pre-recorded video messages, voicemail. **Real within 6 months** for live, interactive video calls at the quality level required to fool an executive over Teams or Zoom. The technology exists in research labs now; commodification into criminal toolkits is the last step, and historically that takes 6-12 months. I would advise planning as if it's a current threat."*
