Slide 6 — Cyber Toolkit Governance Process

This slide is more important than it looks.

It demonstrates that the model is not only conceptually clear, but operationally controlled.

Purpose of the slide

Show that the process is:

repeatable,
proportionate,
governed,
documented,
escalated only where necessary.
Suggested speaking notes

“This is how the model works operationally.

The process starts within RISK Corporate, either as part of onboarding or the annual review. The client completes the standard questionnaire and the responses are assessed against predefined scoring criteria and escalation thresholds.

Straightforward cases remain within the normal RISK Corporate process. Where the results indicate complexity, higher risk or a need for interpretation, the case can be escalated to RISK ORM for specialist support.

The important point here is that escalation is proportionate. We are not trying to subject every client to a bespoke cyber review. The standardised process handles scale, and specialist expertise is focused where the signal warrants it.

The methodology, scoring and escalation criteria are maintained by RISK ORM, while RISK Corporate retains ownership of the client assessment and any resulting credit or client decision.

The process is also embedded into existing credit governance, with dedicated documentation and the ability to address cyber risk formally within credit committees where required.”

Slide 7 — Illustration 1: Cyber Questionnaire

This is where you need to be particularly disciplined.

You have one positive case and one learning case.

Do not frame it as:

success versus failure,
good versus bad,
toolkit worked versus toolkit failed.

That would create unnecessary vulnerability.

The better framing is:

Two cases showing two different ways the framework adds value: one through escalation, the other through learning and refinement.

Suggested speaking notes

“These two examples illustrate two different aspects of the framework in practice.

The first is JD Sports. A significant cyber incident had exposed historical customer data at a time when a major financial transaction was under consideration. Analysis of the questionnaire responses identified areas where further evidence was required, including around independent security assessment, penetration testing and remediation.

That triggered additional challenge and senior review before a decision on the transaction was taken.

The important point is not that the questionnaire made the credit decision. It identified where further evidence and discussion were required.

The JLR example is different. At the time, the subsidiary was not required to complete the questionnaire because group-level assurance was available from Tata Group. Following the subsequent cyber incident, post-event analysis identified material subsidiary-level considerations that were not fully visible through group-level assurance alone.

That case reinforced an important lesson for the framework: for large or operationally distinct subsidiaries, group-level assurance may need to be complemented by entity-specific understanding.

Taken together, the two cases show both sides of the capability: escalation where signals are identified, and refinement of the methodology where experience reveals a limitation.”

That should take around 90 seconds.

Slide 8 — Illustration 2: Sectoral Cyber Intelligence Report
Purpose of the slide

The purpose is to show that:

A cyber control weakness only becomes meaningful when interpreted against the threat, operating model and financial consequences of the sector.

This is where ORM adds real value.

Not by saying:

“this company has weak cyber controls,”

but by helping Credit understand:

why the weakness matters in that sector,
what business disruption it could create,
and what financial questions should follow.
Suggested speaking notes

“The questionnaire gives us a structured view of cyber posture, but on its own that is not enough.

The significance of a particular weakness depends heavily on the sector in which the client operates.

This is why the second part of the capability is sectoral cyber intelligence. It provides the external context needed to interpret questionnaire responses in business and financial terms.

The automotive example illustrates this well. Ransomware is a significant threat in the sector, and the convergence between IT and operational technology means that a cyber event can move very quickly from an information-security issue into a production issue.

So, for a credit officer, a weakness in recovery, continuity or access controls is not simply a technical concern. In this sector, it may indicate a risk of production interruption and therefore potentially material revenue and liquidity consequences.

The sector report also highlights the role of third parties and supply-chain dependencies, as well as the potential importance of insurance coverage.

The objective is not to convert these data points directly into a credit decision. It is to give SCOs the context to ask better questions and to understand when a cyber weakness could become financially material.

And importantly, the same control weakness should not necessarily be interpreted in the same way across every sector. What may represent an acceptable residual risk in one industry may be more material in another where operational resilience expectations or financial consequences are significantly higher.”
